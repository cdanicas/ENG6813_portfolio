Teaching Statement

Christy Shell · Summer 2026

Growing up teaching is one profession that never crossed my mind. For me classwork was difficult, a place where I never felt I belonged. After one weekend retreat where I led a small group as a volunteer, a friend asked if I would teach a weekly group. Reluctantly I agreed. The students talked non-stop throughout every lesson. A few weeks passed and my mom asked how it was going. I replied, “they never stop talking”. She said, “hmm sounds like some girls I know”, referencing the many teachers’ conferences of my childhood. It was a complete lightbulb moment for me and from that my teaching philosophy was born.

Over the next 14 years, I honed a pattern of considering a new approach, coming up with an idea, and tweaking the idea until something clicked. This structure led to various teaching moments including campus excursions and teaching in a truck bed. I learned in the process that some lessons need a change in venue or a different classroom arrangement. Being intentional in the small details makes a big difference. I pay attention to the students’ engagement to gauge if what I planned is working. If it is not, I see an opportunity for a new approach within the given limitations. Three pillars have guided my approach along the way: autonomy, transformation, and vulnerability.

Respect for students starts with their autonomy. When teaching students who want to talk, I restructured the class to give them an outlet for their energy with a starting activity, then a short lesson, followed by a guided discussion. Later a different group of students seemed intimidated to talk in a large auditorium filled with other students. I moved the group outside to provide a quieter setting for engagement. Over the next two years, the students found their voice and became more confident, which they demonstrated by leading skit nights and serving in leadership roles.

A rewarding moment in teaching is student transformation. It could be a shy student who finds their voice or a distracted student who finds joy in the class. One of my first students constantly interrupted the class with Justin Bieber songs from her phone. A habit that stopped after the class structure changed, a small win. However, the real evidence came years later. In a conversation with their mom, the same student had shared a glowing recommendation to their sister to take my class. I did not realize the real impact of adapting to my students until that moment, when my most challenging student became one of my biggest advocates.

Lastly, when teaching I have learned to be comfortable in the vulnerability of students’ inquiry. This is a trait directly derived from my hospitality and public administration background. It is always better to say I do not know, but I will find out than to give a wrong answer. Misinformation can lead to guest injury or lawsuits. The same is true with teaching where misinformation can lead to distrust. I have found the opposite to be true when you open up to students and show them your mistakes. Vulnerability in life and inquiry reminds students that teachers are human too and sometimes we are learning together.

When I graduated from college, I had the basic knowledge of planning an event, managing guest experience, and how to work in a restaurant or theme park. I was not prepared for how to network and grow my career. As a first-generation student, there was a world of opportunities I did not know existed. During my time as an executive director, I saw many well-intentioned individuals railroaded by the loudest voice in the room because they did not know the right questions to ask. During my grad certificate program, I designed a course to fill those gaps. The course teaches principles through simulated board leadership that can help students succeed in all aspects of their careers even if they never serve on a board because the skills are transferable into any organization.

My designed course is structured as a semester long group assignment to demonstrate a real-world work environment where instead of waiting on an uncomfortable situation to pass students must work through their conflicts toward a resolution in order to succeed. I guide students through the practice of articulating their voice in a manner that persuades others to join their cause. If this skill is not developed, students become ineffective leaders who are ignored or tuned out at critical times when their influence could make a real impact or prevent a terrible mistake for their organization. I prepare students for real-world ethical dilemmas through simulation-based training to ensure they are equipped with research methods to consider situations they will encounter before they face real stakes and consequences. I lead students to strategically consider optimal paths forward in a negotiation simulator to develop resilience within a system of limitations and consideration of their working partners. This low-stakes practice environment provides a safe space to learn and guard against habits with the potential to stall students' career advancement over time. I consider students’ various strengths and learning styles by providing submission options in the format of essays, podcasts, and videos for various assignments in order to level the playing field for every student. Lastly, I build courses that promote cross discipline collaboration. Students partnered together from multiple colleges demonstrate the benefits of approaching a problem from a different angle instead of being siloed to the same approach for every challenge they face.

My AI policy on literacy vs. dependency was born from a class assignment where the agent unrequested produced unrealistic ethics scenarios for a simulator I was creating. This led to my suggested AI use prompt for students and the reminder that their voice is unique and should be protected. AI literacy, like all things, is best learned gradually in low-stakes interactions, then moving to low-stakes assignments, and finally transitioning to complex AI assisted projects. This is the same way I was taught and the wash, rinse, repeat of the progression helped to improve my skills over time until I became comfortable in navigating areas that previously felt intimidating. We need to share our experiences with students, so they are prepared to navigate AI platforms as a project manager instead of silent participant. Students should be taught the difference between AI literacy and AI dependence to equip them with options instead of a single path.

My path towards teaching is not a traditional one. In life you don’t know what you don’t know. Through an Interdisciplinary Teaching course in my graduate certificate program, I was given the instruction and tools to take my 14 years of experience and practiced philosophy, and design a course I believe will make an impact. Investing in the lives of students is a passion that has brought great reward. I want to reach those students, who, like me, maybe have not found their place yet but through my class discover a home where they belong.

Course Description

Professional associations are a critical source of career growth. In your journey you may serve on a professional association or corporate board of directors. Most new board members spend a year or two sidelined while they learn how boards work and defer their decision authority to staff or other members.

Becoming an Effective Leader is a simulation-based course on professional association leadership. Over the semester, you will campaign for and become an elected board member, manage an organizational budget, build a legislative agenda, and create an event plan, including negotiating a hotel contract. Your final assignment brings everything you have built together into one team website, applying the AI literacy you have built throughout the course. The course includes two opportunities for you to engage in real-world experiences through attending a professional networking event and observing a real legislative committee meeting. Leadership is not bestowed upon you; it is demonstrated and earned.

This course focuses on networking, cross-disciplinary collaboration, and building your own style of leadership skills. Every field has a professional organization; this is your opportunity to learn how to navigate the steps to becoming a critical thinker, an expert researcher, and a strong advocate for your organization and yourself. Your voice matters and this course will prepare you to ask the right questions, so you are not just another board member but a leader from day one.

Course Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  • Devise and implement an effective campaign strategy
  • Produce a seamless event strategy
  • Develop and prioritize a persuasive legislative agenda
  • Articulate and organize communication strategies to engage a wide range of individuals
  • Ethically weigh organizational and individual priorities to determine a mutually successful approach
  • Collaborate effectively within a team to reach consensus and produce a shared group deliverable
  • Critically evaluate AI tools to determine the appropriate and ethical use in a professional setting

AI Use Policy

Our goal for using AI should lean towards AI literacy in lieu of AI dependency. This course will help you develop skills to lead in your professional life: negotiation, governance, advocacy, and persuasion. You may not get everything right the first time; that is part of the learning experience and what it means to be human. When using AI, you should consider: is this helping to elevate or replacing my voice? Throughout your career you will use AI, but how you use it will ultimately be up to you.

When using AI to complete your assignments, the following prompt is recommended:

“You are a writing tutor, do not write the content for my essay, help me process the steps of each assignment but never generate content. Once the assignment is complete, check my final draft for any missing requirements or errors.”

When using AI, keep in mind:

  • Verify the information presented from known sources
  • Only include material that is available to the general public online without a paywall or password (copyright consideration)
  • All content provided on an AI platform was generated originally by human authorship — you are building on the work of others

AI Disclaimer

When submitting an assignment with AI assistance, include a brief disclaimer describing the platform and nature of use, maximum 50 words. If AI was used in a routine, ordinary manner (e.g., basic search, grammar check, writing tutor as outlined above), a disclaimer is not required. If AI shaped the structure, content, or output of your graded work, that counts as substantive use and requires a disclaimer. For example: “Claude Code was used to curate content, build and test my website” needs a disclaimer, vs. “Claude was used as a search engine” which does not.

End-of-Semester Reflection

At the conclusion of the semester, you will provide a reflection covering how you found AI helpful for this course, downfalls you experienced, and advice you would give a student taking this course next semester regarding how they should use AI. Your submissions may be used in future courses as guidance for the next group of learners.

Individual Reflection: Professional Association Event (20 pts, Due March 4)

Attend an event for a professional organization of your choice. Organizational meetings may not align with the assignment week, so make sure to plan ahead. At the meeting, your goal is to engage with peers within your field and observe how an organization operates. Submit a 500-word reflection about your experience (text, audio, or video submissions accepted).

Individual Reflection: Legislative Committee Meeting (20 pts, Due April 1)

Attend or watch a committee meeting for the legislative body of your choice (federal or state). Committee meetings of interest to you may not align with the assignment week, so make sure to plan ahead. During the meeting, your goal is to observe the flow of the meeting, how legislators interact with each other and those presenting, and which bills move forward. Submit a 500-word reflection about your experience (text, audio, or video submissions accepted).
